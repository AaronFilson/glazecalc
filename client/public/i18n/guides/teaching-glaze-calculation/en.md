## About this pack {#about}

This pack is for anyone who teaches glaze calculation: a school, a studio class, a study group of working potters, or a potter learning alone. It has six sessions, and each one has its aims, what to prepare, a worked example with real materials, how to do it in Glazecalc, a worksheet and questions to check.

The order of the sessions follows a published study group: the 陶磁器釉薬技術研究会 (ceramic glaze study group) of the Ibaraki Prefectural Industrial Technology Center in Japan, which ran six sessions for 12 young potters in 2015 and reported them in its research report. Its follow-on course at the Kasama college of ceramic art set three tile assignments of 42, 90 and 78 glazes, each fired in oxidation and in reduction. **Glazecalc is not connected with the centre or the college.** We follow the order of their published outline, and cite it below; the text, examples and worksheets here are ours. Courses in other countries teach the same steps in nearly the same order: Bilecik Şeyh Edebali University's SCT305 in Turkey, Glendale Community College's ART 195 in the US, the Rhineland-Palatinate syllabus for ceramics schools in Germany, and 李堅萍's 2006 paper for teachers in Taiwan.

### How to use it

- Each session takes about 2 hours. Sessions 4 and 5 need calculators, and a computer or phone with Glazecalc for each pair.
- The worked examples use materials sold in Japan and the figures in Glazecalc's standard library: 福島長石 (Fukushima feldspar), whiting (石灰石, calcium carbonate), a kaolin and silica (珪石). Use your own supplier's analysis where you have one: add it on the [Materials page](/material), and the numbers change, but not the method.
- The firing is SK 8, {{2282 °F; 1250 °C}} nominal. Use the cone your class fires to; [Firing to SK cones](/guides/firing#sk-cones) explains SK numbers.
- To print a worksheet or a tile map, print this page from the browser. Saving recipes, blends and notes needs a free account.

> [!WARNING]
> Sessions 1, 3 and 6 are lab work: weighing dry materials and firing. Before anyone weighs a material, read [Habits that matter most](/guides/safe-mixing#habits). Dry glaze dust is the main hazard.

## Session 1: Materials and triaxial tiles {#materials}

> [!WARNING]
> Weigh dry materials gently, clean up wet, and wear a fitted respirator while the powders are dry. Read [The main hazard: dust](/guides/safe-mixing#the-main-hazard) and [Respirators](/guides/safe-mixing#respirators) first.

### Aims

- Name the three jobs in a glaze: glass former, flux and stabilizer.
- Sort 4 common materials by the oxides they bring.
- Set up a triaxial blend, and read its tiles as a map.

### Prepare

- A bag each of 福島長石, whiting, a kaolin and silica, or the materials your class has.
- The [triaxial tile map](#tile-maps) below, printed, and 21 test tiles per student for each triangle.
- Fired tiles from an earlier triaxial, if you have them, to pass round.

### Worked example

Each material's analysis, from Glazecalc's library, gives the oxides it leaves after firing. Loss on ignition (LOI) is what burns away.

## Session 2: The chemistry you need {#chemistry}

### Aims

- Read a chemical formula: SiO₂, CaO, Al₂O₃, CaCO₃.
- Work out a molar mass from atomic weights.
- Turn grams into moles, and back.

### Prepare

- A periodic table for each student, and calculators.
- The atomic weights in the table below (IUPAC's standard values, as Glazecalc uses them).

### Worked example

Table: Atomic weights used here (g/mol)
Label: Atomic weights

| Element   | Symbol | Atomic weight |
| --------- | ------ | ------------: |
| Oxygen    | O      |        15.999 |
| Carbon    | C      |        12.011 |
| Sodium    | Na     |         22.99 |
| Aluminium | Al     |        26.982 |
| Silicon   | Si     |        28.085 |
| Potassium | K      |        39.098 |
| Calcium   | Ca     |        40.078 |

A formula counts atoms. SiO₂ is one silicon and two oxygen, so one mole of it weighs 28.085 + 2 × 15.999 = 60.083 g. Al₂O₃ is 2 × 26.982 + 3 × 15.999 = 101.961 g. CaO is 56.077 g, and whiting, CaCO₃, is 100.086 g; Glazecalc's library rounds it to 100.08.

A **mole** is a count, like a dozen: the same number of molecules whatever they are. Moles are grams ÷ molar mass:

- 25 g of silica is 25 ÷ 60.083 = 0.416 mol of SiO₂.
- 20 g of whiting is 20 ÷ 100.08 = 0.200 mol. In the kiln it loses its carbon dioxide (CO₂, 44.009 g/mol) and leaves 0.200 mol of CaO, which weighs 0.200 × 56.077 = 11.2 g. That loss is its LOI: 43.97%.

### In Glazecalc

1. Open [Whiting](/materials/whiting) and [Silica](/materials/silica): each page gives the formula and its weights.
2. Check one molar mass by hand against the page.

## Session 3: Reading a unity formula {#reading-the-formula}

> [!WARNING]
> Colouring a glaze means weighing colorants, some of which need more care than the base materials. Read [Materials that need most care](/guides/safe-mixing#materials) first.

### Aims

- Read the three columns of a unity formula (ゼーゲル式, the Seger formula): fluxes, stabilizer, glass former.
- Find a glaze on a silica–alumina chart, and know what the chart can and cannot tell you.
- Colour a ready-made glaze in steps, and record it.

### Prepare

- The unity formula below, written up large.
- A ready-made transparent glaze, and a colorant such as red iron oxide (弁柄).

### Worked example

The glaze of session 4, 福島長石 45, whiting 20, kaolin 10 and silica 25, has this unity formula (Glazecalc's figures, rounded):

Table: A unity formula in its three columns
Label: A unity formula

| Fluxes (RO, R₂O) | Stabilizer (R₂O₃) | Glass former (RO₂) |
| ---------------- | ----------------- | ------------------ |
| K₂O 0.18         | Al₂O₃ 0.43        | SiO₂ 3.45          |
| Na₂O 0.10        |                   |                    |
| CaO 0.72         |                   |                    |

- The fluxes add up to 1: that is the rule of the formula. Each other number is moles for each mole of fluxes.
- Silica to alumina is 3.45 ÷ 0.43, about 8: a typical glossy glaze.
- Calcium is most of the flux, as in most Japanese lime glazes (石灰釉).

A chart with silica across and alumina up places each glaze as a point. Seger's tests and Stull's 1912 chart are of this kind, and a 釉性状図 maps a class's own fired tiles onto it. Glazecalc's recipe page draws the Stull chart, and this glaze sits in its bright region. Stull's regions come from his own fluxes and firing: a guide, not a prediction. The [tile grid](#tile-maps) below lets a class draw its own chart at its own firing.

::stull-chart

To colour a ready-made glaze whose recipe you do not know, Glazecalc cannot work out its formula. Add the colorant on top of the 100 instead: 100 g of the dry glaze with 1, 2 and 3 g of iron oxide, one tile each, fired in oxidation and reduction.

### In Glazecalc

1. On the [recipe page](/recipe), enter the recipe above and read its **Unity formula** and **Stull chart**.
2. Change the silica from 25 to 35, and watch the point move to the right.
3. For a base whose recipe you have, make the colorant an addition and use a line blend on [Blends](/blends) to step it.

## Session 4: From recipe to unity formula {#recipe-to-formula}

### Aims

- Work out a unity formula from a recipe by hand.
- Check it against Glazecalc's own working.

### Prepare

- Worksheet 4, the four analyses from session 1, and calculators.

### Worked example

The recipe: 福島長石 45, whiting 20, EPK kaolin 10, silica 25. Take each amount as grams. For each material, grams × the oxide's percent ÷ 100 ÷ its molar mass gives its moles. The table gives the main oxides; Glazecalc counts the traces too (the kaolin's iron and titanium, the feldspar's magnesium).

Table: Moles of each oxide in the batch (100 g)
Label: Moles in the batch

| Material | Grams |    K₂O |   Na₂O |    CaO |  Al₂O₃ |   SiO₂ |
| -------- | ----: | -----: | -----: | -----: | -----: | -----: |
| 福島長石 |    45 | 0.0514 | 0.0274 | 0.0039 | 0.0862 | 0.4898 |
| Whiting  |    20 |      – |      – | 0.1998 |      – |      – |
| Kaolin   |    10 | 0.0004 | 0.0001 | 0.0003 | 0.0367 | 0.0761 |
| Silica   |    25 |      – |      – |      – |      – | 0.4161 |
| Total    |   100 | 0.0518 | 0.0275 | 0.2041 | 0.1229 | 0.9820 |

The fluxes add up to 0.0518 + 0.0275 + 0.2041 + 0.0014 (magnesia) = 0.2847 mol. Divide every total by it:

- K₂O 0.0518 ÷ 0.2847 = 0.18; Na₂O 0.10; CaO 0.72.
- Al₂O₃ 0.1229 ÷ 0.2847 = 0.43.
- SiO₂ 0.9820 ÷ 0.2847 = 3.45.

That is the formula of session 3.

3. Compare each line with your worksheet. Then change one amount and work out only the column it changes.

## Session 5: From unity formula to recipe {#formula-to-recipe}

### Aims

- Choose materials for a target formula, and work out how much of each.
- Use Solve for a target, and say how near it comes.

### Prepare

- The target below, worksheet 5, and calculators; Glazecalc with an account.

### Worked example

The target: 0.2 K₂O, 0.1 Na₂O, 0.7 CaO, 0.4 Al₂O₃ and 4.0 SiO₂. Take the materials in this order, each for the oxide only it can bring:

1. **The feldspar, for the potash and soda.** One unit of 福島長石 (536.8 g, the weight that holds one mole of fluxes) brings 0.94 mol of K₂O and Na₂O. The target needs 0.3, so 0.3 ÷ 0.94 = 0.319 units: 171.4 g. It also brings 0.015 CaO, 0.328 Al₂O₃ and 1.865 SiO₂.
2. **Whiting, for the rest of the calcium.** 0.7 − 0.015 = 0.685 mol × 100.08 = 68.6 g.
3. **Kaolin, for the rest of the alumina.** 0.4 − 0.328 = 0.072 mol. EPK kaolin holds one mole of Al₂O₃ in 272.6 g, so 19.6 g, which also brings 0.149 SiO₂.
4. **Silica, for the rest.** 4.0 − 1.865 − 0.149 = 1.986 mol × 60.083 = 119.3 g.

The batch is 379.0 g. As percent: 福島長石 45.2, whiting 18.1, kaolin 5.2, silica 31.5. The feldspar sets the ratio of potash to soda, so the recipe gives 0.195 K₂O and 0.104 Na₂O: near the target, not on it.

### In Glazecalc

1. Open [Solve for a target](/solve), choose **A unity formula**, and type the target.
2. Under **The materials**, give it 福島長石, whiting, EPK kaolin and silica, and choose **Solve**.
3. It gives 福島長石 44.9, whiting 18.2, kaolin 5.6 and silica 31.3, and a table of how near each oxide comes. Compare it with your hand working: the solver counts every trace, and finds the best fit the four materials allow.
4. **Open as a new recipe**, and check it on the recipe page.

## Session 6: The clay body and glaze defects {#body-and-defects}

> [!WARNING]
> A fired glaze can release metals into food and drink, and it can look perfect while it does. Glazes high in barium or with copper, and any glaze with lead, can leach. Only a laboratory leach test of the fired pot shows what it releases; no calculation can. Read [Food safety](/guides/glazing-basics#food-safety) and [Materials that need most care](/guides/safe-mixing#materials).

### Aims

- Measure a body's shrinkage and water absorption.
- Name the common defects, their usual causes and a first fix.
- Know which defects make ware unsafe.

### Prepare

- Test bars of your clay body, marked 100 mm apart while wet, fired and unfired.
- Tiles with defects, if you have them: crazing, crawling, pinholes, running.

### Worked example

- **Shrinkage**: (marked length − fired length) ÷ marked length × 100. Marks 100 mm apart that measure 88 mm after firing: 12% shrinkage.
- **Water absorption**: (weight after soaking − dry weight) ÷ dry weight × 100. A fired bar of 50.0 g that weighs 50.6 g after soaking: 1.2%.

Table: Defects, causes and a first fix in the unity formula
Label: Defects

| Defect    | Usual cause                                | A first fix to try                                 |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Crazing   | The glaze shrinks more than the body       | Less K₂O and Na₂O; more calcium, magnesia or boron |
| Shivering | The glaze shrinks far less than the body   | More K₂O and Na₂O                                  |
| Running   | Too little alumina, or too thick           | More alumina; a thinner coat                       |
| Crawling  | Dusty bisque, or a thick coat that cracked | Clean the bisque; a thinner coat                   |
| Pinholes  | Gas that did not heal over                 | A hold at the top of the firing; a thinner coat    |

[Glaze fit and defects](/guides/glazing-basics#fit-and-defects) explains each one, with the ice-water test for crazing. Do not use crazed or shivered ware for food or drink.
